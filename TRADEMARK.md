# Trademark policy

The **code** is AGPL-3.0 — fork it, modify it, run it, charge for it. That
freedom is the point, and this policy does not narrow it.

The **name and the marks** are different. "Common", "Common Network", the
`Common.` wordmark and the logo identify software that comes from this project.
That is the one thing a fork cannot take, because a name that means two
different things means nothing, and users deserve to know whose software they
are running.

## What you may do without asking

- **Say what your software is.** "Built on Common", "a fork of Common Network",
  "compatible with Common" — accurate, factual references are fine, and no
  licence is needed to state a true fact.
- **Run an unmodified Common gateway or node**, publicly or privately, for
  money or for free, and call it Common. It is Common.
- **Write about the project**: articles, papers, talks, tutorials, reviews.
  Academic and journalistic use needs no permission.
- **Use the wordmark and logo** when referring to this project — in a
  presentation, a blog post, a slide about the network you contribute to.

## What you may not do without written permission

- **Name a modified version "Common"**, or any name confusingly similar
  ("Common AI", "CommonNet", "Common Network Pro"). Fork the code, choose your
  own name. This is the ordinary rule for open-source trademarks and the reason
  Firefox forks are not called Firefox.
- **Use the marks as your own** product name, company name, app-store listing,
  domain, or social handle in a way that suggests your project is this one.
- **Imply endorsement, affiliation, or official status** you do not have —
  "official", "certified", "powered by the Common team".
- **Modify the wordmark or logo** and present the result as the project's mark.

## Why this exists alongside a copyleft licence

Common argues against the enclosure of AI, so the code is licensed to prevent
exactly that: a modified version run as a service must publish its changes
(AGPL-3.0 section 13, honoured by this gateway at `GET /source`). The code
stays open to everyone, permanently, including to competitors.

The trademark protects something else — the ability of a user to tell whose
software answered their question. In a network where any machine can join and
any operator can fork, the name is the only thing carrying that meaning. A
policy this ordinary is what keeps "Common" honest, not what makes it closed.

## Asking

Anything not covered here, or a use you are unsure about: open an issue, or
contact Common AI Inc. Requests to use the marks in good faith are usually
granted; we would rather say yes than police edge cases.
